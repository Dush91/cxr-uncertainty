# Introduction

Deep learning systems for chest radiography now match or exceed board-certified radiologists on several thoracic findings [@rajpurkar2018], and the first such products have reached routine clinical use. Their characteristic failure mode, however, is not a visible one. A convolutional classifier confronted with a film it cannot interpret does not abstain, hesitate or produce a diagnostic message: it emits a probability, and that probability looks exactly like the probability it emits when it is right. The system fails silently. In a screening workflow where a model triages hundreds of studies a day and a clinician reviews only what the model surfaces, a silent failure is the one that reaches the patient.

This dissertation is about detecting that specific failure. It is not about improving classification accuracy, and it is not about producing a better-calibrated probability. It is about a narrower and harder question: **given a prediction the model is confident about, can we tell whether it is wrong?** That population — confident predictions — is precisely where calibration has stopped being informative, because within it the predicted probability is by construction near its extreme and no longer varies enough to rank anything. It is also the population that matters operationally, because a confident prediction is the one that gets acted on without a second look.

The work is presented as an MSc dissertation and was also pursued as a commercial endeavour; the two share a codebase and a set of results. The academic contribution reported here is a measurement claim and the experimental design that establishes it. The commercial context shapes the framing only insofar as it forced the deployment question — *would a hospital actually run this, on their own data, and would the uncertainty signal survive?* — to be answered rather than assumed. That question turned out to matter scientifically, and Chapter 5 shows why.

## Background and Context

The standard tool for this problem is *uncertainty quantification*. The dominant decomposition, due to Kendall and Gal [@kendall2017], splits the total (predictive) uncertainty of a probabilistic prediction into an *aleatoric* part, which reflects genuine ambiguity in the image, and an *epistemic* part, which reflects the model's own ignorance and is in principle reducible with more data or better models. The practical estimator of the epistemic part is disagreement across an ensemble of models [@lakshminarayanan2017]: where several independently trained networks give different answers, the ensemble does not know; where they agree, it does. Monte Carlo dropout [@gal2016] is the cheap approximation of the same idea.

The intuition that follows is compelling and is what motivated this project: a *confident* prediction on which the ensemble members *disagree* is a confident prediction that deserves review. Confidence alone cannot flag its own errors, because confidence is symmetric between confidently-right and confidently-wrong; disagreement, being a second-order quantity, might break that symmetry.

That intuition has a documented limit. Abe et al. [@abe2022] show that ensemble members frequently agree on the same wrong answer, which they attribute to shared inductive biases rather than to genuine consensus; the resulting *confident-agree-wrong* cases are invisible to any disagreement-based signal by construction. Ashukha et al. [@ashukha2020] show that in-domain ensembling gains are routinely overstated by weak baselines. And the medical-imaging literature adds its own hazard: chest X-ray benchmarks with a small number of positive cases per class produce estimates whose apparent effect sizes do not survive replication [@oakdenrayner2020].

The most directly relevant recent benchmark, Baur et al. at UNSURE/MICCAI 2025 [@baur2025], evaluates uncertainty methods for chest X-ray classification across four tasks and reports that deep ensembles perform well. Two properties of that study create the gap this dissertation addresses. First, it reports only the *best* of the three ensemble-derived scores — predictive uncertainty, aleatoric uncertainty and epistemic uncertainty — per method per task, which conceals *which* score actually carried each result; "ensembles win" is compatible with the pooled mean winning and the disagreement contributing nothing. Second, its ensembles are same-architecture deep ensembles trained from random initialisation, which is the classical regime but is not the regime in which such systems are now deployed: production systems increasingly combine a small number of heterogeneous pretrained foundation encoders, because that is what is available and what performs.

Those two facts turn out to interact, and the interaction is the scientific core of this dissertation.

## Scope and Objectives

The supervisor's brief specified an agentic system with four components: an error-detection agent, a similar-case retrieval agent, an LLM explanation agent and an improvement-suggestion agent, all runnable on a standard desktop with public models and data. That system was built, and it is the engineering deliverable. The research objectives below state what new knowledge the work aims to produce.

- **O1** — Establish, under a leak-free protocol and at adequate statistical power, whether cross-member ensemble disagreement improves the detection of confidently-wrong chest X-ray predictions relative to pooled confidence.
- **O2** — Determine whether the answer to O1 is governed by the ensemble's *training regime* (pretrained versus from-scratch) or by its *architectural diversity*, by constructing and evaluating the experimental cells that separate the two factors.
- **O3** — Determine whether feature-space density and conformal prediction supply error-detection capability that disagreement cannot, both in-distribution and under distribution shift, and characterise where each layer's guarantee holds and where it breaks.
- **O4** — Produce a similar-case retrieval mechanism whose returned neighbourhood is keyed on the uncertainty flag rather than on raw visual proximity, and quantify the difference this makes to the clinical usefulness of the neighbourhood.
- **O5** — Produce natural-language error explanations and population-level improvement hypotheses in which every claim is mechanically verifiable against computed evidence, and measure how often unconstrained language-model output fails that verification.
- **O6** — Integrate the four agents into a single deployable clinician-facing interface, and evaluate the resulting system against the brief's stated outcomes.

Delivering these required a substantial body of supporting work: curating a patient-disjoint, leakage-audited corpus across five public sources; implementing six ensemble members spanning five distinct representation strategies behind one interface; labelling 129,113 free-text radiology reports with a rule-based clinical labeller; training fifteen models from scratch and adapting six pretrained ones on a GPU host under a data-use agreement that forbade persistence; and building the evaluation battery, statistical machinery and deployment application that the agents run in.

## Achievements

The headline result is a negative that turns into a positive under a controlled decomposition. On a small in-distribution benchmark, feature density appeared to detect confident errors with AUROC 0.87; at proper power on a 160,000-image corpus the same score fell to 0.53, and ensemble disagreement proved statistically indistinguishable from pooled confidence (DeLong *p* = 0.99). Rather than report that as a failure of the method, this work built the missing experimental cells and identified the governing factor: at the operating point that matters, disagreement's value is controlled by **architectural diversity**, not by training regime. Same-architecture ensembles gain +0.05 to +0.14 AUROC from disagreement; architecture-diverse ensembles gain nothing — and an architecture-diverse from-scratch ensemble, built by recombining existing predictions at zero additional compute, reproduces the pretrained deflation almost exactly (0.5585 against 0.5626) despite the opposite training regime. Adapting the pretrained ensemble to the deployment data restores the effect (0.679 against 0.542, *p* = 0.006), which closes the remaining competing explanation.

Alongside that, the work establishes that feature-space Mahalanobis distance is the only score tested that detects confident errors under distribution shift (AUROC 0.818 and 0.649 on two shifted probes with 244 and 313 confident errors respectively), that conformal prediction's coverage guarantee holds in-distribution to within two percentage points and collapses to 0.25 under shift, that keying case retrieval on the uncertainty flag rather than the prediction's argmax raises the clinically relevant precision of the returned neighbourhood from 0.014 to 1.000, and that a mechanical faithfulness audit rejects both of the language-model renderings produced in a live trial, each of which a human reader would very likely have accepted.

## Overview of Dissertation

Chapter 2 reviews the background theory and the literature, working from calibration through selective prediction, conformal prediction and feature-space out-of-distribution detection to case retrieval and grounded language generation, and closes by stating the gap. Chapter 3 describes the system's design and the data curation, including the leakage controls and a probability-scale defect whose diagnosis shaped the rest of the work. Chapter 4 presents Agent 1 and the in-distribution and shifted-data results that motivated the pivot to a powered benchmark. Chapter 5 is the research core: the powered evaluation, the from-scratch control, the cross-backbone decomposition that separates regime from diversity, and the adaptation experiment. Chapters 6, 7 and 8 present Agents 2, 3 and 4 respectively. Chapter 9 covers integration, the deployed application, testing and the professional-practice dimension including data-governance compliance. Chapter 10 evaluates the work against the objectives above and sets out future work.
