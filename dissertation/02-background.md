# Background Theory and Literature Review

This chapter establishes the theory the rest of the dissertation depends on and positions the work against the literature. It proceeds from the general to the specific. Section 2.1 sets out what it means to evaluate a probabilistic prediction well, and why no single number suffices. Section 2.2 develops the uncertainty decomposition and its ensemble estimator, together with the documented reasons that estimator can fail. Section 2.3 covers calibration and its rank-invariance, which is the property that separates a calibration problem from a ranking problem. Section 2.4 covers selective prediction and the metric this dissertation is organised around. Section 2.5 covers conformal prediction; Section 2.6 covers feature-space out-of-distribution detection. Section 2.7 reviews the chest X-ray models and corpora used. Section 2.8 covers case-based retrieval, and Section 2.9 covers language-model explanation and the faithfulness problem. Section 2.10 summarises the clinical and regulatory setting, and Section 2.11 states the gap this work fills.

## Evaluating a probabilistic prediction

Gneiting and Raftery [@gneiting2007] give the organising principle: the goal of probabilistic forecasting is to *maximise sharpness subject to calibration*. A forecast is calibrated if, among the cases it assigns probability *p*, a fraction *p* are positive; it is sharp if its probabilities are concentrated away from the base rate. Calibration without sharpness is achieved trivially by always predicting the prevalence, so both are needed.

The modern uncertainty literature adds a third axis that the forecasting framing does not name explicitly: **discrimination**, or ranking. The question "does this score separate the wrong predictions from the correct ones?" is distinct from both calibration and sharpness, and a model can be excellent at one and useless at another. This dissertation's defining metric lives on the ranking axis, and Section 2.3 shows why that placement makes the usual calibration remedies inapplicable to it.

Each axis has standard instruments. On calibration, the expected calibration error (ECE) bins predictions by confidence and averages the absolute gap between bin accuracy and bin confidence [@guo2017; @naeini2015]. ECE is convenient and badly behaved: it is sensitive to the binning scheme, biased upward at small sample sizes [@roelofs2022], and can be driven to zero by a forecaster that is calibrated but uninformative [@vaicenavicius2019]. Nixon et al. [@nixon2019] propose equal-mass adaptive binning and class-wise variants as partial repairs. This work reports ECE at four bin counts (5, 10, 15 and 20) precisely so that a binning artefact is visible rather than hidden.

On sharpness, proper scoring rules are the principled instrument. The Brier score admits Murphy's decomposition [@murphy1973] into reliability minus resolution plus uncertainty, which separates "the probabilities are honest" from "the probabilities are informative"; negative log-likelihood is the other standard choice. Both are reported here, and the Murphy identity is asserted as a unit test.

On discrimination, the relevant instruments are described in Section 2.4. The baseline against which any such score must justify itself is the maximum softmax probability [@hendrycks2017] — a baseline repeatedly found harder to beat than expected, and which appears throughout this dissertation under the name *pooled confidence*.

## Uncertainty decomposition and its ensemble estimator

For a binary decision with predictive distribution obtained by averaging *M* member probabilities, the Kendall–Gal decomposition [@kendall2017] gives three quantities. Writing the pooled mean as the average of member probabilities, the *predictive* (total) uncertainty is the entropy of the pooled prediction; the *aleatoric* part is the average of the members' individual entropies; and the *epistemic* part is the difference, which is the mutual information between the prediction and the model parameters:

$$ PU = H[p̄] ,   AU = (1/M) Σ H[p_i] ,   EU = PU − AU $$ {#eq:ch2-kg}

Equation {{eq:ch2-kg}} is what the system computes for every image–pathology pair. Alongside mutual information, this work also reports the cross-member standard deviation of the probabilities, which is a different estimator of the same epistemic concept and is the one the project was originally built around. The distinction matters for reading the literature: reporting "the ensemble's uncertainty" without saying which of these three scalars is meant leaves the result ambiguous, and Section 2.11 shows this is exactly what happens in the closest prior benchmark.

Deep ensembles [@lakshminarayanan2017] estimate the epistemic term by training *M* networks from independent random initialisations; the members reach different modes of the loss landscape [@fort2019] and their disagreement is taken as a proxy for parameter uncertainty. Monte Carlo dropout [@gal2016] approximates the same object far more cheaply by sampling dropout masks at inference. Both estimators require that the members genuinely differ where the model is wrong.

Two documented failure modes bound the approach and both are directly relevant here. First, Abe et al. [@abe2022] show that ensemble members share inductive biases and consequently agree on a substantial set of errors; those *confident-agree-wrong* cases cannot be flagged by any disagreement statistic, because there is no disagreement to measure. Second, Ashukha et al. [@ashukha2020] show that ensembling gains reported in-domain are systematically inflated when the single-model baseline is weak or uncalibrated, and argue for comparing against a properly calibrated baseline at matched conditions. This dissertation takes both warnings as design constraints: pooled confidence is treated as a first-class competitor rather than a strawman, and the irreducible agree-wrong core is measured rather than assumed away. A third warning, from Ovadia et al. [@ovadia2019], is that every uncertainty method degrades under dataset shift and that their relative ranking changes with it, so a method validated in-distribution cannot be assumed to transfer; Chapter 4 measures that degradation directly on two shifted probes.

A third consideration, less often stated, concerns the *type* of diversity. Cross-seed disagreement within one architecture and cross-architecture disagreement between different backbones are not the same signal: the former varies where the training data is ambiguous, the latter where inductive biases differ. Nothing in the literature reviewed here separates them empirically for error flagging. Chapter 5 does.

## Calibration and rank-invariance

Temperature scaling [@guo2017] divides the logits by a single scalar fitted on a held-out calibration split. It is the standard post-hoc calibrator, it is effective, and it has a property that is decisive for this work: because it is a strictly monotone transform of the logit, **it cannot change the ranking of predictions by confidence**. Any metric that is a function of ranking alone — AUROC among them — is therefore invariant to temperature scaling.

This has a sharp consequence. If a confident-error detection result is poor, temperature scaling cannot repair it; the problem is not that the probabilities are dishonest but that the score does not order errors above correct predictions. Conversely, if calibration and ranking disagree, that is information about which axis is failing. Chapter 4 uses this as a diagnostic: the observation that ECE improves tenfold while the confident-error AUROC is unchanged localises the problem to the ranking axis, and the observation that a *different* calibrator (per-member Beta calibration) destroys the ranking localises the signal to the inter-member probability scale.

Beta calibration and other per-member schemes differ from a single pooled temperature in one respect that turns out to matter: applied per member, they normalise away the scale differences *between* members. If those scale differences are part of what makes cross-member disagreement informative, per-member calibration removes the signal while improving the reported calibration number. Section 4.4 reports exactly this.

## Selective prediction and the defining metric

Selective prediction [@elyaniv2010] equips a classifier with the option to abstain. Given an uncertainty score, one abstains on the most uncertain fraction and measures the error rate on what remains; sweeping the abstention rate traces a risk–coverage curve whose area (AURC) summarises the score's quality. Because AURC depends on the classifier's base error rate as well as the score, Geifman et al. [@geifman2019] introduce the excess AURC (E-AURC), which subtracts the value an oracle score would attain and thus permits comparison across models of different accuracy. Ding et al. [@ding2020] and Corbière et al. [@corbiere2019] make related points about failure prediction being a distinct task from calibration.

This dissertation's defining metric is a restriction of that framework. Rather than measuring how well a score orders errors over the whole population, it measures how well the score orders errors **within the confident stratum**. Concretely: take the top *k*% of predictions by calibrated confidence, and compute the AUROC of the uncertainty score for separating the wrong predictions in that stratum from the correct ones. This is the *confident-error AUROC*, and Section 4.2 gives its exact implementation.

The restriction is what makes the problem hard and what makes it worth studying. Over the full population, confidence is a strong error score almost by definition — low-confidence predictions really are more often wrong — so a full-coverage metric largely rewards whichever score best approximates the pooled mean. Within the confident stratum, confidence has saturated: every prediction there is near-extreme, and confidence has little variance left with which to rank. That is the regime in which a second-order signal such as disagreement could add something, and it is the regime in which a deployed triage system operates. Chapter 5 shows that the two regimes give opposite answers about which score to use, and that this is not a contradiction but a consequence of where each score's dynamic range lies.

## Conformal prediction

Conformal prediction [@vovk2005; @angelopoulos2021] converts any point predictor into a set-valued predictor with a distribution-free finite-sample coverage guarantee: given exchangeable calibration and test data and a target level 1 − α, the produced set contains the truth with probability at least 1 − α, with no assumption about the model. For classification, the Least Ambiguous Set-valued Classifier (LAC) [@sadinle2019] thresholds the softmax score directly and yields the smallest average set size at a given coverage; adaptive prediction sets [@romano2020] trade size for better conditional coverage.

Marginal coverage is a weak guarantee: it holds on average over classes, and can be satisfied while a particular class is badly under-covered. *Mondrian* conformal prediction restores per-group validity by calibrating a separate threshold per group — here, one per pathology. That is the variant implemented in this work.

The critical caveat is exchangeability. Under distribution shift the calibration and test distributions differ, the exchangeability assumption fails, and the guarantee is void — not degraded gracefully, but void. Chapter 4 measures how void: coverage moves from 0.896 in-distribution to 0.269 and 0.245 on two shifted probes, and, worse, the produced sets remain size-one, so the system continues to auto-read predictions it is now getting wrong without ever referring them.

## Feature-space out-of-distribution detection

An alternative family of scores ignores the output layer and works in representation space. The Mahalanobis detector [@lee2018] fits class-conditional Gaussians to penultimate features and scores a test point by its minimum Mahalanobis distance to a class centroid; with high-dimensional features the covariance estimate needs shrinkage, for which the Ledoit–Wolf estimator [@ledoit2004] is the standard choice. Energy-based detection [@liu2020energy] instead uses the log-sum-exp of the logits. Deep nearest-neighbour detection [@sun2022knn] replaces the parametric density with the distance to the *k*-th nearest training feature, which avoids the Gaussian assumption.

For medical imaging specifically, Woodland et al. [@woodland2024] find that non-parametric *k*-NN distance generally outperforms Mahalanobis, because medical feature distributions are not well described by a single Gaussian per class. The results in Chapters 4 and 5 reproduce both this ranking and the repeated finding that energy-based scores underperform badly on medical data.

An important distinction, often blurred, separates two questions these scores are used for. *Is this image out of distribution?* is a comparatively easy question, and Section 4.7 reports AUROCs of 0.98–0.99 for it. *Given that this image is out of distribution and the model is confident, is the model wrong?* is much harder, and the same scores achieve 0.65–0.82. Conflating the two overstates what a density score delivers operationally.

## Chest X-ray classification: corpora and encoders

Four public corpora anchor the field. NIH ChestX-ray14 [@wang2017] supplies 112,120 images labelled by natural-language processing over reports into fourteen findings, which is the ontology adopted here. CheXpert [@irvin2019] adds 224,316 images and, importantly, an explicit *uncertain* label produced by its rule-based labeller. MIMIC-CXR [@johnson2019] supplies 377,110 images with free-text reports. Open-i [@demner2016] is a smaller, fully open collection used here for in-distribution evaluation. ReXGradient-160K [@rexgradient2025], released in 2025, supplies 160,000 studies with reports and — because it postdates every pretrained encoder used in this work — provides a corpus that is leak-free by recency rather than by curation. Kermany et al.'s paediatric pneumonia collection [@kermany2018] and the COVID-19 Radiography Database [@rahman2021] serve as distribution-shift probes.

These corpora carry known hazards. Their labels are derived from reports by rule-based tools such as NegBio [@peng2018negbio] and CheXbert [@smit2020] rather than from image review, so label noise is structural; Oakden-Rayner [@oakdenrayner2020] documents substantial disagreement between NIH labels and expert re-reads. Models trained on them are prone to shortcut learning — Zech et al. [@zech2018] show a pneumonia model keyed on hospital-specific markers, and DeGrave et al. [@degrave2021] show the same for COVID classifiers. Both hazards argue for leak-free splits and for external evaluation, which Chapter 3 implements.

On the encoder side, torchxrayvision [@cohen2022] distributes DenseNet-121 [@huang2017] classifiers trained per-dataset, providing a set of genuinely independently trained members. Beyond them, four modern families are used here: ConvNeXt-V2 [@woo2023], a masked-autoencoder-pretrained convolutional network; RAD-DINO [@perezgarcia2025], a DINOv2-style [@oquab2024] self-supervised vision transformer trained on radiographs without text supervision; Ark+ [@ma2023ark], a Swin-Large [@liu2021swin] encoder cyclically pretrained across six supervised chest X-ray corpora; and BiomedCLIP [@zhang2023biomedclip], a biomedical image–text contrastive model. Adapting such encoders is usually done by linear probing followed by fine-tuning, which Kumar et al. [@kumar2022lpft] show preserves out-of-distribution performance better than fine-tuning alone.

## Case-based retrieval

Retrieving similar prior cases is an old idea in clinical decision support and a natural complement to an uncertainty flag: a clinician asked to review a flagged prediction is better served by comparable prior films than by a saliency map. Approximate nearest-neighbour search over learned embeddings makes this practical at scale; hierarchical navigable small-world graphs [@malkov2020] give logarithmic-time search with high recall, and FAISS [@johnson2021faiss] is the alternative standard implementation.

The design question that matters clinically is not the index but the *key*. Nearest neighbours in a visual embedding space are visually similar films, which is not the same as clinically relevant comparators for a specific flagged finding. Chapter 6 shows that keying retrieval on the flagged pathology rather than on the prediction's argmax changes the returned neighbourhood's relevance by two orders of magnitude, and that naively enlarging the reference library made one important query class *worse* before a dedicated retrieval lane fixed it.

## Language-model explanation and faithfulness

The final component turns the flag and its neighbourhood into prose. Recent radiology work is dominated by vision–language models that read pixels: MAIRA-2 [@bannur2024maira] performs grounded report generation in which findings are tied to image regions, and ChestX-Reasoner [@fan2025chestx] adds process supervision so that intermediate reasoning steps can be verified. GREEN [@ostmeier2024green] supplies an error taxonomy — hallucination, omission, incorrect location, incorrect severity, incorrect comparison — for critiquing generated reports.

The governing hazard is hallucination [@ji2023hallucination]: a fluent, confident, unsupported claim. In a system whose entire purpose is to flag confident unsupported claims by a classifier, a language component that makes confident unsupported claims of its own is self-defeating. This motivates the design used in Chapters 7 and 8, in which the language model is not permitted to read pixels, receives only a structured evidence pack with addressable identifiers, is instructed to cite an identifier for every factual claim, and has its output mechanically checked against that pack before a human ever sees it. This trades capability for verifiability, deliberately and measurably: Chapter 8 reports how often the check fires.

More broadly, the system is agentic in the practical sense surveyed by Wang et al. [@zhou2024llmagents] — specialised components with distinct scopes, composed so that one component's structured output is another's input — rather than in the sense of an autonomous planner such as ReAct [@yao2023react]. Section 9.2 argues that this is the appropriate choice for a clinical safety tool, where the composition needs to be auditable rather than emergent.

## Clinical and regulatory context

Two facts about the deployment setting shape what a contribution here can claim. First, regulators evaluate discrimination, not uncertainty: NICE's early value assessment of AI for chest X-ray lung-cancer detection [@nice2024] and the FDA's 2025 draft guidance on AI-enabled device software [@fda2025] are framed around sensitivity, specificity and lifecycle management, with uncertainty-awareness emerging as good practice but not as a binding requirement. Second, the operational pressure is real — the Royal College of Radiologists reports a persistent UK reporting shortfall [@rcr2024] — but naive worklist prioritisation has not straightforwardly delivered, which is why abstention-based triage, where the system defers rather than reorders, is the direction of travel.

The honest framing that follows, and the one adopted throughout this dissertation, is that a confident-error flag is a **safety and workflow feature** attached to a product that will be evaluated on discrimination. It does not improve the AUROC of the underlying classifier and is not claimed to. Its value is fewer confident errors reaching a clinician unexamined, and an auditable record of why each deferral happened. Related to this, Rudin [@rudin2019] argues that post-hoc explanations of black boxes are the wrong tool for high-stakes decisions, a position sharpened by Caruana et al.'s demonstration [@caruana2015] that a high-accuracy pneumonia risk model had learned a clinically dangerous rule which only an interpretable model exposed. The standard post-hoc tools — Grad-CAM saliency [@selvaraju2017] and Shapley attributions [@lundberg2017] — explain *where* a network looked, not whether it was right, and are therefore not instruments for the question this work asks. The response taken here is not to claim interpretability of the classifier but to make the *uncertainty layer's* reasoning fully auditable, which is a weaker and achievable goal. Leibig et al. [@leibig2017] provide the closest precedent for uncertainty-driven referral in medical imaging.

## Summary

The literature supplies a well-developed toolkit — an uncertainty decomposition, its ensemble estimator, rank-invariant calibration, selective-prediction metrics, distribution-free conformal coverage and feature-space density scores — together with clear warnings about ensemble agreement on errors [@abe2022], inflated in-domain gains [@ashukha2020] and small-sample artefacts in medical benchmarks [@oakdenrayner2020].

What it does not supply is an answer to the question this dissertation asks. The closest benchmark [@baur2025] reports the best of predictive, aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty per task, so it cannot say whether disagreement or the pooled mean carried its result; and it evaluates only same-architecture from-scratch ensembles, so it says nothing about the architecture-diverse pretrained ensembles that are now deployed. Neither does any work reviewed here separate cross-seed diversity from cross-architecture diversity as an explanatory factor, or measure the confident-error flag at a sample size large enough for the estimate to be stable.

The gap, then, is threefold: **disentangle** which ensemble-derived score carries which task; **measure** the confident-error flag at adequate power under a leak-free protocol; and **decompose** the result along the regime and diversity axes to identify what actually governs it. Chapters 3 to 5 close that gap; Chapters 6 to 8 build the agentic system that consumes the resulting flag.
